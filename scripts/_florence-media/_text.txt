A Florentine Lunch: The Search for the Perfect Schiacciata

From Medieval Palace to Prison to Museum
---P54 drawings=0
The building itself is as fascinating as the collections.
---P55 drawings=0
Constructed in the mid-13th century, the Palazzo del Bargello is one of Florence's oldest surviving civic buildings.
---P56 drawings=0
It originally housed the Capitano del Popolo, a powerful official appointed to represent the interests of the city's citizens, and later became the seat of the Podestà, Florence's chief magistrate.
---P57 drawings=0
Over the centuries, the palace acquired a much darker role.
---P58 drawings=0
It became the headquarters of the Bargello, the city's chief of police, and served as a prison. Its courtyard, now one of the museum's most beautiful spaces, was once associated with public punishments and executions.
---P59 drawings=0
In 1865, following restoration, the building opened as the Museo Nazionale del Bargello, becoming Italy's first national museum.
---P60 drawings=0
Today its medieval architecture provides an extraordinary setting for one of the world's finest collections of Renaissance sculpture.

Donatello, Michelangelo and the Birth of Renaissance Sculpture
---P63 drawings=0
One of the museum's greatest treasures is Donatello's bronze David.
---P64 drawings=0
Created in the 15th century, it is generally considered the first freestanding nude male statue cast in bronze since antiquity.
---P65 drawings=0
The youthful David stands with one foot resting on Goliath's severed head, his expression strangely contemplative rather than triumphant.
---P66 drawings=0
It was a revolutionary work, demonstrating how Renaissance artists were rediscovering the human body and classical sculptural traditions.
---P67 drawings=0
Nearby, Donatello's marble David reveals how his approach developed over time.
---P68 drawings=0
The Bargello also contains two remarkable bronze reliefs of the Sacrifice of Isaac, created by Lorenzo Ghiberti and Filippo Brunelleschi for the famous 1401 competition to design new doors for Florence's Baptistery.
---P69 drawings=0
Seeing the two panels side by side is fascinating.
---P70 drawings=0
Ghiberti's elegant, harmonious composition ultimately won the competition, while Brunelleschi's version is more dramatic and forceful.
---P71 drawings=0
The competition became one of the defining moments in the early history of the Florentine Renaissance.
---P72 drawings=0
Then there is Michelangelo.
---P73 drawings=0
His Bacchus, carved when he was still in his early twenties, depicts the Roman god of wine with a wonderfully convincing sense of unsteady movement.
---P74 drawings=0
Unlike the idealised, heroic figures associated with much classical sculpture, this Bacchus appears almost drunk enough to topple over.
---P75 drawings=0
The museum also houses Michelangelo's Brutus and the beautiful Tondo Pitti, a circular marble relief of the Virgin and Child.
---P76 drawings=0
And beyond these celebrated works are extraordinary collections of glazed terracotta by the della Robbia family, small bronzes, ivory carvings, enamelled objects and decorative arts.
---P77 drawings=0
We found ourselves lingering over details that would be easy to overlook in a larger, busier museum.
---P78 drawings=0
The Bargello is a wonderful reminder that Renaissance Florence wasn't simply a city of painters. It was also home to some of the most inventive sculptors, metalworkers and craftsmen in European history.
---P79 drawings=0
Allow at least 90 minutes, preferably two hours.

The Brancacci Chapel: Where Renaissance Painting Changed Forever
---P96 drawings=0
Tucked inside the Church of Santa Maria del Carmine is one of the most important artistic treasures in Florence.
---P97 drawings=0
The Brancacci Chapel may be relatively small, but its influence on Western art has been enormous.
---P98 drawings=0
Its frescoes were begun in the 1420s by Masolino and his younger collaborator Masaccio, before being completed decades later by Filippino Lippi.
---P99 drawings=0
What makes the chapel so significant is the extraordinary transformation in the way human figures were painted.
---P100 drawings=0
During the Middle Ages, religious painting had often emphasised symbolism over realism. Figures could appear flat, idealised and detached from their surroundings.
---P101 drawings=0
Masaccio changed that.
---P102 drawings=0
He gave his figures convincing weight and physical presence, placing them within architectural spaces that obeyed the new principles of linear perspective.
---P103 drawings=0
Light falls consistently across bodies and buildings. Faces reveal genuine emotion. People seem to occupy real space.
---P104 drawings=0
The result was revolutionary.
---P105 ALLBOLD drawings=0
Adam and Eve Like You've Never Seen Them
---P106 drawings=0
One of the chapel's most powerful paintings is Masaccio's Expulsion from the Garden of Eden.
---P107 drawings=0
Adam covers his face in anguish while Eve cries out, her expression conveying an extraordinary sense of shame and grief.
---P108 drawings=0
It's a deeply human scene, quite unlike the more formal religious imagery that preceded it.
---P109 drawings=0
Nearby, The Tribute Money demonstrates Masaccio's mastery of perspective and narrative.
---P110 drawings=0
The fresco tells three episodes of a biblical story within a single continuous landscape, with Christ and his disciples arranged around a central scene.
---P111 drawings=0
Its architectural depth, naturalistic figures and carefully controlled light helped establish principles that generations of Renaissance painters would follow.
---P112 drawings=0
Michelangelo is known to have studied the frescoes, as did many other artists who came to Florence to understand the new Renaissance approach to painting.
---P113 drawings=0
Standing inside the chapel, it's remarkable to think how much of Western art history was influenced by this relatively small space.

The Extraordinary Life of Artemisia Gentileschi
---P170 drawings=0
Born in Rome in 1593, Artemisia was the daughter of painter Orazio Gentileschi, who recognised her talent and trained her in his workshop.
---P171 drawings=0
From an early age, she demonstrated an exceptional ability to depict dramatic scenes, strong emotions and the human figure.
---P172 drawings=0
Her style was profoundly influenced by Caravaggio, particularly his use of dramatic contrasts between light and darkness.
---P173 drawings=0
But Artemisia's path to becoming a professional artist was extraordinarily difficult.
---P174 drawings=0
In 1612, she became the central figure in a highly publicised trial after accusing painter Agostino Tassi of rape. The proceedings subjected her to humiliating questioning and physical torture intended to test her testimony.
---P175 drawings=0
Tassi was convicted, although the punishment imposed was not meaningfully enforced.
---P176 drawings=0
For centuries, Artemisia's extraordinary artistic achievements were too often overshadowed by this episode.
---P177 drawings=0
Yet what happened next is just as important to understanding her life.
---P178 drawings=0
Artemisia moved to Florence, where she began building an independent artistic career in a society that offered very few opportunities to women.
---P179 drawings=0
She developed relationships with leading intellectuals and artists, received commissions from the Medici court and became the first woman admitted to Florence's prestigious Accademia delle Arti del Disegno in 1616.
---P180 drawings=0
Her paintings are remarkable for their psychological intensity, powerful female subjects and masterful handling of light.
---P181 drawings=0
Works such as Judith Slaying Holofernes are uncompromising in their physical realism and emotional force, while others reveal her ability to depict tenderness, vulnerability and quiet reflection.
---P182 drawings=0
Artemisia was not simply an exceptional female artist working against the odds. She was one of the outstanding painters of the Italian Baroque.

Why This Exhibition Is Worth Seeing
---P185 drawings=0
The exhibition focuses on Artemisia's years in Florence, a period that was fundamental to her artistic development and professional independence.
---P186 drawings=0

---P187 drawings=0

---P189 drawings=0

---P190 drawings=0
The exhibition also offers a valuable reminder of the women whose contributions to art history have not always received the recognition they deserve.
